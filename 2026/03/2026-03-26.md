**Legal exposure:** If a chatbot's stored data is subpoenaed and used against a survivor in court (custody loss, restraining order denial), **liability questions emerge**. **What "privacy-by-default" means in practice:**
1. **Ephemeral mode** — conversations auto-delete after session ends (no server logs)
2. **Local processing** — sensitive queries run on-device, never sent to cloud
3. **Explicit consent** — users must opt-in to data retention, not opt-out

**Why companies resist:** Data = model improvement = competitive advantage. Every conversation trains the next version. Privacy-by-default means **sacrificing billions in training data**. OpenAI, Google won't do this voluntarily — only regulation forces it.

**Bottom line:** This is AI's **hidden harm** — not dramatic failures (Sora shutdown) or hype bubbles (SMCI crash), but **quiet erosion of privacy** that disproportionately harms vulnerable populations. ### 🏛️ Supreme Court Showdown: Ottawa Clashes with Provinces Over Notwithstanding Clause

**Federal government pushes for limits on Charter override power; Ontario and Alberta push back**
The federal government and provincial heavyweights **clashed at the Supreme Court** on Wednesday over potential restrictions on the **notwithstanding clause** — the constitutional escape hatch that lets governments override Charter rights (Globe and Mail, 5h ago). Ottawa wants the court to impose **first-ever limits on repeated use** of the clause and allow judges to issue declarations when laws violate rights, even if the clause prevents overturning those laws.

**What's at stake:** The notwithstanding clause (Section 33) lets governments temporarily override fundamental freedoms, legal rights, and equality guarantees. It's supposed to be a **democratic safety valve** — but increasingly, provinces use it as a **legislative sledgehammer** to bulldoze through court challenges. Quebec's Bill 21 (religious symbols ban), Ontario's back-to-work legislation, and Alberta's transgender policy bills all invoked Section 33.

**Federal vs Provincial power grab:** Ottawa frames this as **protecting Charter rights** from provincial overreach. Ontario and Alberta see it as **federal interference** in provincial jurisdiction. The real question: **Who decides when rights can be overridden?** The federal government wants courts to have final say (via declarations). Provinces want legislative supremacy. This is a **fundamental constitutional battle** disguised as legal procedure.

**Why this matters beyond politics:** If the Supreme Court sides with Ottawa and restricts Section 33 use, provinces lose a critical tool for **fast-tracking controversial policies**. If the court sides with provinces, the notwithstanding clause becomes **normalized as standard legislative practice** — not a rare exception, but routine governance. That fundamentally weakens the Charter's protections.

**Bottom line:** This case rewrites the balance between **judicial review and legislative power** in Canada. Expect a decision in 6-9 months that will shape Canadian governance for decades.
